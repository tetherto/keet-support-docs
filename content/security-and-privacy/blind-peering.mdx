Keet is [**serverless**](/#how-does-keet-work-without-central-servers) — there's no company server sitting in the middle, storing your chats. So a fair question is: **if I send a message and the other person is offline, where does it go?**

The answer is **blind peering**, and it's how Keet quietly puts the *"server"* back in *serverless*. Some peer still does the one job a server used to do — stay online and hold your message until the other person is back — except no company owns it and it can never read what it's holding.

## The problem, in one sentence

For a message to arrive, *someone* has to be online to pass it along. But the person you're messaging is offline — and a moment later, you might go offline too. So who holds the message in the meantime?

## The simple version

Think of your message as a **sealed envelope, written in a code only the person you're messaging can read**.

To reach your friend, that envelope gets passed to other people already connected to the network — think of them as **couriers**. A courier will happily **hold onto the envelope** while your friend is away and **keep passing it along** until it arrives. Because the envelope is sealed and coded, **no courier can read what's inside** — they only know to carry it and hand it on.

In Keet, those couriers are **blind peers** (sometimes called *blind relays*). They're just other computers on the network that agree to hold your message and pass it along.

## Why is it called "blind"?

Because the helper is **blind to what's inside**.

Your message is [**end-to-end encrypted**](/#is-keet-encrypted) *before* it ever leaves your device. The blind peer only ever sees a scrambled, locked blob of data. It knows *"hold this and forward it,"* but it can **never read your message, your name, or who it's for** in a way it can understand.

<Callout type="info">
A blind peer isn't a company server. It's just another peer on the peer-to-peer network — anyone can run one, and no single company owns or controls your messages. 🌐
</Callout>

## Putting it together

So when you message someone who's offline — and then go offline yourself before they're back — here's what happens:

1. You wrote a message. It was **encrypted on your device**.
2. Because your friend was offline, the encrypted message was **held by one or more blind peers** on the network.
3. You went offline — but that's fine, because **you were no longer the only one holding it**.
4. When your friend reconnected, they **synced the encrypted message from a blind peer** and their device unlocked it.

Those four steps look like this end to end:

So the message *was* stored somewhere — but only ever as **locked, unreadable data on other peers**, never as readable text on a company server. 🔐

<Callout type="warn">
No plain-text copy of your message exists anywhere outside you and the person you're talking to. The blind peer only ever holds the encrypted version. ⚠️
</Callout>

## Want the deep technical version?

This page is the friendly summary. Under the hood, blind peering is really about **data availability** on Keet's peer-to-peer stack (Hypercore and Hyperswarm): a blind peer is a dedicated replicator that **stores and serves your encrypted data without ever decrypting it** — giving you server-like availability without a server.

For the full technical explanation, see [Availability and blind peering](https://docs.pears.com/explanation/availability-and-blind-peering/) 🍐 in the Pear documentation.
